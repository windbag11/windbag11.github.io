the fruits of cineville

A recent revolutionising development in my life has been me acquiring my Cineville pass. What this is, is essentially a pass where I pay 19 eur a month to be able to watch an unlimited amount of movies at all the arthouse theaters in the Netherlands. And this has set forth for me what I call my cinephile era, where I’ve been watching so, so, so many movies, many that I would not have previously even thought about watching, and quite spontaneously as well, so that if I have a free evening after a whole day of work, I can quickly look through the local cinema’s agenda for the day for films that look interesting, book a seat for free maybe half an hour before the movie starts, bike the 7 min ride it takes to the filmhuis, get myself either a glass of pinot noir or warme chocomel at the bar, scan my ticket, sit down and unwind. Honestly, getting Cineville has also upgraded how I spend time with myself, and also with other people, and my days feel so much livelier when I have such a place to take myself out to.

So this post is one that I will hopefully keep adding onto, with a little review of every film I choose to watch with Cineville, with my thoughts on them.

Paris, Texas - Wim Wenders (1984)

Starting with Paris, Texas. This movie is one that I watched before I acquired Cineville, but it was definitely a big factor to me getting it. I watched this film on my own spontaneously after a weekend of isolating myself after summer break and going a bit insane in my room. It was time I got outside the house and took myself out on a solo-date, in a quite high-strung, emotional mind state. Wim Wenders, the director, had directed another movie I had really loved, Perfect Days, which is about the daily life of a toilet cleaner in Tokyo. His capturing of the mundane and making it so so beautiful but also so familiar was something I wanted to relive through another movie of his. This was what I wrote in my notes app after I stepped out:

Honestly, cinematographically, a perfect movie. That confessional two way mirror scene was so powerful and just all the scenes with Travis and Hunter like them in their pickup or them walking on two sides of the street, or just Travis walking straight on, and all the landscapes etc were so beautiful. Also emotionally really engaging like when Travis was talking about his parents and how could relate it with how he felt about Jane how he built this image of her. I think the transition between him being mute and essentially a child to him being fully functioning was a bit abrupt, but the small scenes of how he wanted to look like a father were really heartwarming. Harry’s and Kinski’s acting were spectacular, especially his hidden smiles. Yeah Travis is a fucked up person, abusive, possessive and also a pedophile with how he got with Jane but I think the fact that he chose to not stay with the two no matter how much he felt emotionally about Hunter or about Janes life now, shows how he knows or regrets the possibility that he’s going to fuck up again and loves them both too much to subject that to them. Wenders was talking about how some distributers wanted him to change the last scene into him taking a U-turn as he left, but Wenders chose not to because then Travis is just a coward and not accountable for his actions, and I’m so happy he made that decision. I cried a lot at all the fatherly scenes and especially that last dialogue between Harry and Natassja. Spectacular.

Blue Velvet - David Lynch (1986)

Another movie I watched pre-Cineville, but oh my, what a movie. I decided to watch this movie with two friends, after I watched Mulholland Drive (and a youtube video explaining whatever the fuck that movie was about) and I was actually hit by awe of Lynch’s genius. When watching Blue Velvet, I was hit by, one, just the beauty of every shot, but two, also the seemingly very simplistic and absurd plot going on on the surface? And also just how every scene felt like an important symbol that you had to pay attention to, because if you even blink you might miss something that was the key to decoding whatever Lynch wanted to say. My friend’s interpretation of the movie was that Jeffrey had been a victim to sexual abuse in the past, and watching Dorothy be abused, and afterwards himself, was something his mind was creating to deal with his trauma. The ending where everything becomes perfect, almost paradisiacal, is a symbol of how good wins in the end, how Jeffrey faces and shoots his trauma, or Frank, head-on, and gets over his past and lives happily ever after. My impressions were a bit different but followed a similar storyline, where I thought that this film was about Jeffrey facing the possible fact that he himself was actually a monster, depicted in this story as Frank, also especially with that scene when Jeffrey hits Dorothy, but he finally chooses to be a good person, and then gets his happily ever after. Anyhow, what a beautiful film, with the most haunting and breathtaking film score that plays neverendingly in the back of my mind, and how beautiful and fucked up was Isabella Rossellini as Dorothy.

Also as a side note, apparently Lynch was falling in love with Isabella at the production of this movie, when he was still married to Mary Fisk. And while Mary Fisk was obviously disappointed and resentful of David doing this, she still said that she had so much respect for David Lynch and his artform, because to make art like this, one has to experience and interact and know hismelf with love, and so, with someone like David who is so familiar with the internal workings of his own mind, so familiar that he does the impossible task of actually putting it on a screen for us to see and a catch a glimpse of, one has to consider David as someone superhuman. As a genius who interacts and shares and feels love in a profoundly different way than I can imagine. And how incredible of Mary Fisk to be able to say this about her ex-husband who had a whole affair when married to her. To be able to set aside your own hurt and your grief and still appreciate David’s artistic genius is not something many people can do.

Akira - Katsuhiro Otomo (1988)

A classic. A must watch honestly on the big screen. And my first watch with Cineville. It’s astounding honestly when you get lost in the plot and suddenly you remember that every frame you are watching, amongst the 24 you come across in a second, has been hand drawn and painted in, down to the tiniest little lighted windows that sit on one of the hundreds of skyscrapers that decorate the skyline of cyberpunk Tokyo. Stunning, and frankly, I can’t ever meaningfully grasp at the amount of admiration I have for animators and the effort they put in a single piece of work. Plotwise, the story was kind of everywhere at once, following many more characters than I would have prefered. But wow, the animation made up for everything a thousand times.

Teenage Sex and Death at Camp Miasma - Jane Schoenbrun (2026)

Wow, what even. This movie I felt is not something I like because of the movie itself, but more because of what it was kind of getting at. Like nothing about the characters or the plot or the symbolism or anything was something worthy of mentioning (except maybe Gillian Anderson) but the nuance and layers as you peel back the story was what reminded me of why I love storytelling. I’m not sure if this nuance I’m getting from it is actually intentional or another classic example of me projecting my own world view or recent thoughts onto the message of a certain form of media, but anyhow, this is what I wrote:

Teenage Sex & Death at Camp Miasma - Gillian Anderson is so hot. I feel like this movie was about voyeurism and performance, both in terms of sex and also the act of acting. You become aware that what you desire is an audience to everything you do ultimately and then you confront that desire, play it out, humour it and then maybe even laugh at it in its face, and then you realise that even the act of grappling with that desire and facing it is finally also a performace in the end, a cohesive narrative, and that you never actually escape the movie. It could be us as the audience, or the male gaze, or anything, but we’re always performing. Dialogue a bit cringe and very gen-z stereotyped but I think the movie was also making fun of that? I need to try that dipping sauce. Alternative synopsis - a two hour tutorial from Gillian Anderson on a new orgasm strat. She is so hot.

Reading this back makes me wonder how much I project the fears and thoughts I obsess over onto whatever I take from anything I consume. Brings me back to Blue Velvet where I thought the story was about Jeffrey realising his inner monster, which was something I thought a lot about subconsciously for myself at that time. Also after this movie I had a three hour long discussion about performativeness and how hard finding a true sense of joy or feeling in simple actions or hobbies had gotten to be because of this self awareness. I guess this is what film is meant to do, act as a place of escapism where you can also project your real life onto why a specific film sticks with you.

Trainspotting - Danny Boyle (1996)

Spectacular. I don’t know how Boyle made a movie with such depressing scenes so fucking funny, but he did it alright. I felt quite scummy and repulsed in that toilet scene. The overdose scene with Perfect Day playing in the background was honestly such a mind muffling, breath heaving kind of scene, I have no words. Trainspotting refers to when Scottish druggies would go to derelict train stations to shoot up. But what it also refers to is the very mundane activity of train spotting, where you watch trains with hundreds of souls pass by you. all going about their day, in love, growing up, growing older, while you stay glued to the same place, chasing that one moment of euphoria that brings everything else in your life to a halt. I ended up listening to the soundtrack for the next two weeks after watching this movie. Boom, diggy-diggy-diggy, boom, diggy, bang.

Kokuho - Sang-il Lee (2025)

Not that interesting, definitely very pretty, but there was no reason this movie had to be 3 hours long. I felt absolutely no sympathy for the characters, and the plot was just not engaging at all. What was interesting though was what I learnt about kabuki - a Japanese dance and art form that originated with a woman, but then was performed by little boys when women were banned from performance arts because of its association with sex work, and then finally by adult men when the little boys were banned as well. And honestly, I would’ve been happy if the film was about the main character and his journey with kabuki, but what it was in the end was this confused limbo that alternated between him and his life as a kabuki actor, and then him and his relationships with other people, without ever properly getting into the nitty gritty of either. Lead actor was very good looking.
